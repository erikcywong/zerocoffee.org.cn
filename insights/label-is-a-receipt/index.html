Labels · verification

A label is a receipt, not a certificate

A certificate says somebody approved you. A label should say what was measured, by whom, against which rules — and let anyone recheck it.

ZERO CARBON VERIFY · ESTABLISHMENT NOTE · 5 MIN READ

Most carbon labels are decoration with a logo. They assert a conclusion and withhold the working, which makes them impossible to audit and trivial to fake.

What a label actually contains

The labels we issue carry fourteen fields and a hash. The fields are the claim; the hash is the mechanism that stops the claim being edited after the fact.

Field groupWhat it holdsWhy it is there
Identitylabel id, batch, issue date, expiryCan be tied to physical goods
Methodversion, hash, boundary, GWP setTwo labels are comparable only on the same method
Resultper-unit, total, baseline, reductionThe number and the denominator
Qualitymetered share, grade, intervalHow much is measured rather than assumed
Provenanceverifier, status, liabilitiesWho signed and what they are allowed to claim

The hash is the point

Each label commits to its payload and to the previous label's hash:

hash = fnv( prev_hash + batch | total | per_unit | grade )

That structure makes the register append-only. You cannot quietly revise a number without breaking every label after it, and anyone with the published fields can recompute the chain themselves. Our own server is a convenience, not an authority.

What this is not

  • Not a carbon credit. Nothing on our labels is tradable, and we will not describe it as an offset.
  • Not an accreditation. Until the body is accredited, every document says so.
  • Not a quality mark. A low-carbon plantlet can still be a bad plantlet. Different question, different liability.

Try it on the register

The verification page recomputes the chain in the browser, with no server call. If you change a digit in the payload, the check turns red — which is the only demonstration of integrity that is worth anything.
