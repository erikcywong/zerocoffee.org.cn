Economics · scale

Why 60,000 plantlets never pays back a self-build

The smallest economic unit in this business is not a farm. It is a cluster of eight to fifteen farms sharing one edge box.

ZERO CARBON VERIFY · ESTABLISHMENT NOTE · 7 MIN READ

Every hardware business in agriculture hits the same wall: the farm is too small to own the stack that makes it efficient, and too small to be worth the sales cost of serving it.

The arithmetic for one farm

Item60,000 plantlets / yr240,000 plantlets / yr
Loss avoided per year¥28,000¥112,000
Zone cost (seat + per plantlet)¥3,450¥8,850
Self-build annual holding cost¥34,000¥34,000
Self-build paybacknever~4 years

The self-build number is dominated by the edge box: about ¥18,000 every three years, plus power, connectivity and somebody's time. Divided across 60,000 plantlets it is ¥34,000 a year of cost against ¥28,000 of avoided loss. Divided across eight farms it is ¥750 each.

So why not one box per village?

Because a box serves a zone of roughly 72,000 plantlets before latency and connectivity stop being free. The break-even sits at about 72,857 plantlets a year — above that, owning is cheaper; below it, sharing is.

The cluster is the unit

This is why the commercial model is a zone rather than a farm: the box is shared, the telemetry is pooled, and the per-farm price lands at a level a smallholder will actually pay. It also means the sales motion is cooperative, not one farm at a time.

The numbers above are a cost model, not measured outcomes. They assume the avoided loss figure holds, which depends entirely on the decision layer being right about something the farmer cares about.

What this means for a verifier

A cluster of small farms looks like one client and many sites. Verification is priced per site, so the arithmetic that makes the cluster work commercially also makes verification expensive per farm — unless the sampling is designed around the cluster rather than the site.
